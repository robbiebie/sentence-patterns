Unit 01 • Grammar Focus

Relative Clauses

Relative clauses add essential or extra information about a noun without starting a completely new sentence. They help make your academic writing flow smoothly and sound more sophisticated.

Quick Overview

A relative clause connects two ideas by describing a person, thing, place, or time using relative pronouns like who, which, that, whose, where, or when.

Mastering relative clauses helps you combine short, repetitive sentences into polished complex sentences in your essays.

Key Rules to Remember

  • Defining: Essential info; NO commas; can use that.
  • Non-Defining: Extra info; MUST use commas; CANNOT use that.
  • Omission Rule: You can drop who/which/that ONLY when it acts as the object of the clause (e.g., The book (that) I read).
  • Using "Where" (Places): Where refers to a place and MUST be followed by a complete sentence (Subject + Verb) (e.g., The school where I studied). If the clause lacks a subject and starts directly with a verb, use which (e.g., The school which is nearby).
ADVANCED USE Academic Focus: Preposition + Relative Pronoun

In formal writing and academic essays, prepositions are placed directly before which or whom rather than dangling at the end of the sentence:

Everyday / Spoken: The topic that we were talking about...
Formal / Advanced: The topic about which we were talking...
Everyday / Spoken: The tools that he conducted experiments with...
Formal / Advanced: The tools with which he conducted experiments...

Sentence Showcase

1. Defining (Essential) The student who won the debate scholarship spoke at the assembly.
2. Non-Defining (Extra info with commas) My laptop, which I bought last month, runs all high-end graphics apps.
3. Pronoun Omitted (Object Position) The podcast (that) we listened to yesterday was super inspiring.
4. Place with "Where" (Followed by complete sentence) That is the laboratory where the research team conducts experiments (Followed by complete sentence: 'the research team conducts experiments').
5. 🌟 Advanced Use (Formal Prepositions) This is the environment in which endangered species thrive, and the cause for which they raised funds.

Unit 02 • Advanced Syntax

Participle Clauses

Participle clauses allow you to express information about condition, cause, time, or result in a concise way using -ing, -ed, or having + past participle.

What are Participle Clauses?

They are shortened adverbial or relative clauses. They make prose punchy and are common in journalism and literature.

Golden Rule: The subject of the participle clause MUST be the exact same as the subject of the main clause!

Three Key Forms

  • Present Participle (-ing): Active meaning happening simultaneously (e.g., Feeling confident, she spoke up).
  • Past Participle (-ed/V3): Passive meaning (e.g., Built in 1990, the bridge is sturdy).
  • Perfect Participle (Having + V3): Action completed earlier (e.g., Having finished the essay, Liam slept).

Sentence Showcase

1. Active / Simultaneous (-ing) Walking into the laboratory, the scientist noticed the reaction immediately.
2. Passive Meaning (-ed / V3) Surrounded by supportive friends, Maya felt ready for the challenge.
3. Completed Prior Action (Having + V3) Having saved enough money from his internship, Alex bought a new guitar.

Interactive Skill Builders

Participle Clauses Quiz

3 Questions
⚠️
Beware of Dangling Participles: Always ensure the main subject performs the action in the participle clause!
❌ Dangling: Walking down the street, a tree branch fell on me. (The branch wasn't walking!)
✅ Correct: Walking down the street, I was hit by a falling tree branch.
Unit 03 • Clause Functions

Noun Clauses

A noun clause is a dependent clause that acts as a single noun. It can serve as a subject, direct object, or subject complement in a sentence.

What triggers a Noun Clause?

Noun clauses usually begin with words like that, whether, if, or wh-words (what, how, why, where, who).

If you can replace the entire clause with the pronoun "it" or "something", it is almost certainly a noun clause!

Grammatical Functions

  • Subject (Main Idea): What you said made everyone rethink the strategy.
  • Direct Object (After Action Verb): I wonder if the test will be rescheduled.
  • Sentence Completion (After 'is' / 'was'): The truth is that we need more practice.

Sentence Showcase

1. That-Clause (Object) The principal announced that modern laptops would be assigned to all seniors.
2. Wh-Clause (Subject Position) Whatever you decide to present will be supported by the group.
3. If/Whether-Clause (Embedded Question) The coach asked whether we were ready for the final match.
